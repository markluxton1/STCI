# 2. The simultaneous geometry of F and G

Write \(X=V(F)\), \(Y=V(G)\), with generic orders \(r=\operatorname{ord}_{C_0}F\), \(s=\operatorname{ord}_{C_0}G\). On \(B=\operatorname{Bl}_{C_0}\mathbf P^3\), the strict-transform divisor classes are \(dH-rE\) and \(eH-sE\). The exceptional divisor is \(\mathbf P(N_{C_0/\mathbf P^3})\), hence abstractly \(\mathbf P^1\times\mathbf P^1\).

At the generic point of the curve, the first normal forms \(F_r,G_s\) are binary homogeneous forms. If they are coprime, the local intersection multiplicity equals \(rs\); when they share a factor, excess contact must be studied at higher orders. **A common factor does not by itself assert equality of first derivatives** when either order exceeds one.

The chronological research record contains a common-normal-factor argument and the associated resultant bounds (see [research record](../RESEARCH_RECORD.md), including P-006). Its exact hypotheses should be checked before applying it to an arbitrary new pair. The recent work on the blowups of \(C_0\) and \(Z=V(F,G)\), their common resolutions, and the exceptional common-factor curve is a promising geometric lane, **not yet a separately promoted canonical theorem**.

Two distinct problems emerge: (i) describe the common divisor of the first symbols on the exceptional ruled surface, including degeneration over special points; (ii) measure the remaining excess intersection by successive transforms, Rees valuations, or an equivalent intrinsic invariant. These problems connect to, but are not settled by, the root-scheme criteria of Chapter 3.
